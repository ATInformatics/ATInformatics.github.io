Dissertation

A computational neuroscience project, taken from an immunology and biochemistry degree rather than a neuroscience one: sleep architecture in young against old mice, and how it maps onto human sleep. I worked from EEG and EMG recordings, electrodes reading brain tissue and muscle tissue, scored the records into wake, REM and NREM, then extracted and combined those stages and fitted probability curves across several statistical methods.

02

What it taught me, and what I do with it.

I can take a scientific paper apart

This is what four years actually bought me, and it was not facts. I can read a difficult paper, work out what was done and why that design rather than another, and judge whether the conclusion is carried by the data or by the discussion.

The field I want to work in

Machine learning applied to the immunology literature and to single-cell data: Domínguez Conde and colleagues in the Teichmann lab at the Wellcome Sanger Institute, whose Science paper of May 2022 resolved 101 immune populations across 16 tissues using CellTypist, their own automated classifier; the Human Cell Atlas it feeds; and the immune-cell knowledge graphs now being built over the published record to surface connections nobody made and point at experiments worth running. That is pattern recognition on a corpus, which is the work I already do in another field.

I analyse data to a deadline, for a client

A dissertation teaches the method. Paid work teaches the rest: a dataset too large to look at, a question that has to be answered from it, and an output somebody else can check.

I work to a protocol, and I write them

A laboratory teaches you that a procedure which is almost right is wrong, and that a result you cannot repeat is not a result. Everything the company builds now reads back what it wrote instead of reporting success. One product reconciled 364 of 364 figures against what a client had actually filed, not against its own output.

I carry deadlines, and other people's time

Improv, records with a release date, and a company run by one person all teach the same lesson: the work is rarely the hard part, the sequencing is. Several things in flight, people waiting on each of them, and a date that does not move.

I plan the work before I write it

Agent and AI work punishes you hardest for skipping this. I turn a job into steps with the check that closes each one, and I write that down first. It is the same instinct as a method section.

I learn a regulated domain properly

Bulgarian tax and payroll law, modelled closely enough to produce declarations a client files with the revenue office. Reading the statute is the job. The code is the easy part.

I instrument a field rather than only working in it

ITU-R BS.1770 loudness measurement and loudness-matched blind comparison, implemented from the published standard. Knowing a subject well enough to build its instruments is a different thing from being able to use them.
